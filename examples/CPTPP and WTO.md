## CPTPP and WTO — Regional vs Universal Trade Cooperation

The World Trade Organisation has 166 members and normally makes major decisions by consensus. This gives it near-universal reach, but agreement becomes difficult when states with very different economic interests must all accept the same rules. Rather than waiting for WTO-wide agreements, a smaller group of states created the Comprehensive and Progressive Agreement for Trans-Pacific Partnership (CPTPP), which now brings together 12 economies, including Japan, Singapore, Australia, Canada and the UK. Within this more selective grouping, members have agreed to much deeper trade liberalisation; for example, after joining, more than 99% of current UK goods exports to CPTPP members became eligible for tariff-free trade.

**Use**: What this shows is that countries do have the option of obtaining benefits without waiting for agreement from all WTO members, reducing the WTO's exclusivity and centrality as the forum for negotiating trade liberalisation. With such international organisations no longer being indispensable as the main venue for advancing trade, it is no wonder that some may come to see international organisations as being irrelevant.

While CPTPP shows why smaller groupings can sometimes cooperate more deeply than a universal international organisation, this does not make the WTO irrelevant. The CPTPP applies only among its members, whereas the WTO has 166 members accounting for around 98% of global trade. Its rules therefore govern commercial relations across a much wider set of countries. Even in 2026, about 72% of global merchandise trade was still conducted on the WTO’s core most-favoured-nation terms, meaning that much of world trade continues to depend on the common baseline established by the WTO. Moreover, arrangements such as the CPTPP actually operate within the wider WTO framework: WTO rules expressly permit regional free-trade agreements under specified conditions, and members must notify such agreements to the WTO.

**Use**: What is clear is that regional mechanisms supplement rather than replace universal IOs: they may reduce the WTO’s exclusivity as a venue for liberalisation, but not its relevance as the common foundation of the global trading system.
